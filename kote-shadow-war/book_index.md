# Shadow War — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index of *Shadow War* (White Wolf, 1999, by Edward MacGregor and Bryant Durrell), a *Kindred of the East* supplement dedicated to inter-Kuei-jin conflict. For exact quotes, precise rules numbers, or specific named NPCs, query `book_chunks.db` (see README.md) rather than relying on the summaries below.

## Overview
*Shadow War* is a book about how Kuei-jin fight each other — and, in its final chapter, about a specific war against the Kindred. It formalizes three escalating tiers of conflict: **twilight war** (ritualized, non-lethal contests of skill and strategy, mediated by a neutral Ganshezhe), **midnight war** (all-out, ceremonially-declared vendettas with real casualties, bound by the Six Strictures of War), and **underground war** (the unsanctioned guerrilla struggle of the youth movement known as the Bamboo Princes against the elder establishment). A chapter on Scarlet Screens covers the mortal organizations Kuei-jin use as tools of both business and war. The book closes with a full chapter, **Crimson War**, detailing an actual ongoing war the Kuei-jin have launched against the Kindred Anarchs of California — extending the setting into the West — and an appendix of artifacts, rites, and named "Gods of War" (famous shadow-war practitioners).

## Introduction: Welcome to Life After Midnight (pp. 5-7)
**Summary:** Frames shadow war as a necessary safety valve — formalized conflict that keeps Kuei-jin infighting from destroying Kuei-jin society and the Middle Kingdom outright. Defines shadow war as any formal conflict short of total war, ideally hidden from mortal and outside view. Previews all chapters and gives an extensive two-part lexicon: formal military/ceremonial terms (Addressing the Heavens/Earth/Hells, Beigao, Ganshezhe, Gathering/Breaking of Clouds, Lianfang, Tanwu, Xuezhe, Yangao, etc.) and "Running Monkey-speak," the slang of the underground war movement (Bamboo Princes, Dame-sama, Ichimi, Jiang Hu, Zuboshi, etc.).
**Key themes:** Ritualized conflict as social safety valve; formal vs. informal registers of war.
**Key terms/entities:** Twilight war / midnight war / underground war distinction (p.6); full lexicon (pp.6-7).

## Chapter One: Twilight War (pp. 9-28)
**Summary:** Covers the most common, least lethal form of shadow war — non-lethal, honor-based contests resolved under a neutral mediator (the Ganshezhe). Gives a history: honorable combat among the Wan Xian, the heretical **Shan-bo Heresy** (censured by the Quincunx for questioning combat norms), the tradition of **Sworn Brotherhoods** and the legendary **Three Golden Egrets** (Fa-tang, Jin, and Jing, friends before their elevation to Wan Xian, whose duel is recorded as the First Wan Xian Duel), and the arrival of the Kuei-jin proper. Details **The Three Books of the War in Twilight** — foundational texts governing declaration (First Book), proper conduct including the use of **Xiang-qi** (elephant chess) as a formalized medium for settling disputes (Second Book), and the role and Five Qualities of the Ganshezhe mediator (Third Book). Illustrates the system with several in-character vignettes (Chan Ai-ling declaring war on her court; Qi-chen mediating a war between Liu and Kon) and surveys regional variations in the Green Courts and Golden Courts. Closes with tactical advice (New Moves, Surprise Allies, Courting the Court, Losing with Elegance) and multiple named NPC perspectives on twilight war (Ae-Cha Soo Hyun of the Blood Court, Kanou Makoto, Woo Sung Ju-il of Seoul's Bamboo Princes, and a visiting Toreador emissary, Sidra Masters).
**Key themes:** War as art and etiquette; honor-bound conflict resolution as an alternative to Kindred anarchy.
**Key terms/entities:** Ganshezhe (mediator); Xiang-qi/elephant chess as war-medium (p.581 area); Three Golden Egrets; Shan-bo Heresy; named voices — Ae-Cha Soo Hyun, Sidra Masters (Toreador).

## Chapter Two: Midnight War (pp. 29-50)
**Summary:** Covers the lethal, all-out tier of shadow war, reserved for grievous offenses. Explains the **Three Laws of Yuan-Te**, which constrain when midnight war may be declared, with regional variants (Golden Courts, Green Courts of Korea). Details the full **Midnight Ceremonies** sequence: Addressing the Heavens (declaration, at midnight on a new moon), the intervening "Addressing the Earth" period (including deceptive "Feint Praise" tactics), and Addressing the Hells (the closing rite, one lunar month later) — plus special procedures for a **War on the Akuma**. Lays out **the Six Strictures of War** (rules of conduct, e.g. prohibitions on involving the Yama Kings or striking noncombatants) and other laws of war. Surveys regional "Midnight Theaters" with illustrative vignettes (War in the World of Swords and Mountains; an "Overkill" cautionary tale involving a Uighur mandarin and the Blood Court's "Eyes of Jade"; War in the Hearts of Warriors and Farmers). Covers **War Across the Realms** — using spirit-world resources and allies (Nectar of the Other Worlds; risks of angering Yang spirits or Emperor Qin Shihuang of the Yellow Springs) and **Midnight Stratagems**, including forbidden maneuvers like bartering with Kin-jin or visiting the Yomi Wan in Hiruko, the "Hell of Unspeakable Delights," for aid. Closes with the Fundamentals of Strategy (morale, logistics, leadership factors), the Rudiments of Deceit, the Bonds of Honor, Life Among the Troops, and post-war ceremony (Praising the Heavens), illustrated by the vignette "The Tale of Okui Megumi and Okui Miyu."
**Key themes:** War as codified ceremony even at its most lethal; the danger of demonic shortcuts (dealing with Yama Kings) as a recurring temptation and taboo.
**Key terms/entities:** Three Laws of Yuan-Te; Midnight Ceremonies (Addressing the Heavens/Earth/Hells); Six Strictures of War; Hiruko/Hell of Unspeakable Delights; Emperor Qin Shihuang.

## Chapter Three: Underground War (pp. 51-74)
**Summary:** Covers the informal, unsanctioned guerrilla conflict waged by the **Bamboo Princes** (Takeouji in Japanese, Zhu Wangzi in Chinese) — a revolutionary movement of young Kuei-jin ("Running Monkeys") seeking to modernize Kuei-jin society against resistant elders/mandarins ("dame-sama," "Eunuchs"). Covers the movement's roots in **Jiang Hu** rebellious gang culture, its founding association with the figure **Burakumin** (who coined the name Takeouji), organizational structure (**Ichimi** cells, ranks of Zuboshi/Shifuku/Heimin), and relations with other factions — Westerners/gaijin shen, Kin-jin (with a "Name That Kin-jin" identification guide), demons. Gives Bamboo Prince tactics against elders (Standard Operating Procedure, the historical "Bell of Zhi Bo" cautionary tale, counterintelligence, "Old Age and Treachery"), internal politics (the "Secret Masters of the Bamboo Princes," the **Prophesy of the Jade Snake**, "Family Affairs," "Playing the Game"), and a practical "Quick and Dirty Guide to Survival" for young Kuei-jin (Hit the Books, Get Connected, Things to Do with Chi).
**Key themes:** Generational conflict as an organizing axis of Kuei-jin politics; youth movements/modernization vs. tradition, paralleling the core book's Ch.1 hint at this tension.
**Key terms/entities:** Bamboo Princes/Takeouji (p.532); Burakumin; Ichimi (cell); Zuboshi/Shifuku/Heimin ranks; Prophesy of the Jade Snake.

## Chapter Four: Scarlet Screens (pp. 75-82)
**Summary:** Covers the mortal organizations ("Scarlet Screens") Kuei-jin maintain as tools for both mundane business and shadow war. Traces the practice's origin back to the Wan Xian's mortal retinues in the Third Age (ministers, treasurers, warriors) and explains how the term "Scarlet Screen" itself entered common usage at the end of the Fourth Age. Categorizes Screen types — Family (e.g. the Miura of Kyoto, an arranged-marriage and "chilling" — assassination — operation), Soldiers (ranked from Triumphant Hares up through sarugumi "monkey gangs," illustrated by Khuc Nguyet's "Dancing Lightnings" unit), Support Troops, Spies (e.g. Yamaguchi Investigations), Merchants (e.g. the Western-sounding but Kuei-jin-run McNamara and Delancey Imports), Artisans (e.g. Atorie no Shiroihoshi, a Genji-affiliated studio), and Peasantry. Closes with advice on maintaining a Screen's loyalty ("Keeping the Screen," "The Heart of the Servant").
**Key themes:** Mortal instrumentalization as a structural feature of Kuei-jin society; the Scarlet Screen as both economic and military infrastructure.
**Key terms/entities:** Scarlet Screen categories — Family, Soldiers, Support Troops, Spies, Merchants, Artisans, Peasantry; named examples — the Miura of Kyoto, Khuc Nguyet's Dancing Lightnings, Yamaguchi Investigations, McNamara and Delancey Imports, Atorie no Shiroihoshi.

## Chapter Five: Crimson War (pp. 83-95)
**Summary:** A detailed treatment of a single ongoing conflict: a sudden Kuei-jin offensive against the Kindred Anarch Free States of California. Opens with a framing story set beneath Beijing's Underground City, where Ancestor Ch'ang of the Blood Court interrogates della Passaglia, an Italian Kindred conducting forbidden research into the Second Breath. The chapter proper covers: the history of Kuei-jin/Kindred contact (from Marco Polo's era through early colonial contact, e.g. an anecdote involving "Prince Roderigo"); how the Camarilla and Sabbat currently perceive the Kuei-jin ("Today's Secrets"); systematic points of confusion Westerners have about Cathayans (the Courts, the Dharmas, P'o vs. the Beast); practical guidance on "Battling the Children of Caine" (physical conflict, relative numbers, political strife, use of the spirit realms); an overview of "Those Who Take" — the Five August Courts and other courts now operating in North America; and a region-by-region breakdown of the Crimson War's front lines — Los Angeles (incl. Mandarin Hao and the newly named Monkey Trip Wu), San Diego, "Points North," Hawaii, Australia and the Pacific, South and Central America, and Europe (incl. a brief anecdote involving "Prince Wilhelm").
**Key themes:** East-vs-West conflict escalated to open war; the Kuei-jin's expansion beyond the Middle Kingdom as a Fifth Age development, directly continuing the core book's Ch.7 "Kuei-jin in the West" storytelling guidance.
**Key terms/entities:** Ancestor Ch'ang, Blood Court; della Passaglia (Kindred researcher); Anarch Free States (California); Monkey Trip Wu, Mandarin Hao (Los Angeles); regional fronts — Los Angeles, San Diego, Hawaii, Australia, South/Central America, Europe.

## Appendix: Gods of War (pp. 96-102)
**Summary:** A grab-bag of war-related game material. **Artifacts of War**: the **wu shi** ("five stones"), egg-sized jade Chi-storing amulets said to represent the 33 handmaids of the Scarlet Queen (nine known to survive; the named/famous one is **Ai, Peacock Mistress of the Eight Bells**, held by Hsu Chong-yee of Taipei). **Rites of the Shadow Warriors**: ritual game-mechanical rites, e.g. the Rite of the Gracious Courier (Level One). **"Gods of War"**: profiles of legendary/iconic shadow-war figures grouped by war-type — Gods of Twilight War (Pak Yung-lee), Gods of Midnight War (Sun Li, Zhao Rao "Dowager Mother of Demons," Tien Lu), and Gods of the Underground War (Burakumin, "Herald of the Revolution," plus Junko Inoue and Yuu Doi).
**Key themes:** Naming/mythologizing famous practitioners as a worldbuilding device, paralleling the core book's approach to elder Kuei-jin.
**Key terms/entities:** Wu shi (artifact); Ai, Peacock Mistress of the Eight Bells; Burakumin; Sun Li; Zhao Rao.

## Cross-cutting themes
- **Three-tiered war system** (twilight/midnight/underground) is the book's core structural contribution, extending the core rulebook's brief mention of "shadow war" (Ch.1) into a full ceremonial and tactical system.
- **Generational conflict**: the Bamboo Princes (Ch.3) directly continue a tension only hinted at in the core book and expanded in the Companion (young vs. old Kuei-jin, tradition vs. modernization).
- **The West as battlefield**: Crimson War (Ch.5) is the most concrete depiction in this library of Kuei-jin operating outside the Middle Kingdom, directly continuing the Companion's Ch.6 "Kuei-jin Abroad" material into an active war narrative.
- **Named courts and figures recur across the library**: the Blood Court (Ancestor Ch'ang) and the Five August Courts appear here as in the Companion's Ch.1; Bishamon and Genji (Companion Ch.1) are referenced again in the Underground War chapter's "Lay of the Land."
