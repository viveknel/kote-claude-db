# Wind from the East — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index of *Wind from the East* (White Wolf, 2000, by James Kiley), a sourcebook for *Vampire: The Dark Ages* covering the Mongol Empire. For exact quotes, precise rules numbers, or specific named NPCs, query `book_chunks.db` (see README.md in this bundle) rather than relying on the summaries below. Page numbers below are the book's own printed page numbers (see README.md for the offset against the chunk database's PDF page numbers).

## Overview
*Wind from the East* is a *Vampire: The Dark Ages* sourcebook (not a *Kindred of the East* line title, though it draws heavily on that line and its historical companion *World of Darkness: Blood & Silk*) covering the Mongol Empire from Temujin's birth (c. 1166) to the fall of the Yuan Dynasty (1368) and beyond. Its central move is introducing the **Anda**, a long-hidden offshoot bloodline of Clan Gangrel native to the Mongolian steppe, and their unofficial rivals the **Wu Zao** (an Eastern offshoot of Clan Salubri) and the **Wan Kuei of the Black Tortoise Court** (the Cathayan vampires who claim the northern steppe as their own). The book opens with a short fiction piece (A Deliberate Encounter), then moves through a full narrative history of the Mongol conquests with vampiric politics woven throughout (Ch.1), detailed writeups of the Anda, Wu Zao, and Black Tortoise Court (Ch.2), new game systems and Traits for Mongol-era play (Ch.3), and an appendix of ready-to-use characters (Appendix). Its stated theme is "the bright candle" — the Mongol Empire, and the Anda bloodline whose fate is tied to it, burn twice as hot and half as long.

## A Deliberate Encounter (pp. 6-9)
**Summary:** A fiction vignette narrated by an unnamed Anda (Mongol Gangrel) vampire who infiltrates the city of Bolgar just ahead of the Golden Horde's 1236 invasion, hunting for information from the Western ("Far West") vampires he's heard infest such cities. After a fruitless first night, he interrogates and drains a "grossly fat, somewhat pale" Western vampire in an alley, learning that the creature serves a mortal ruler ("Prince Hasan") and fears the coming Mongol horde will sack Bolgar as it did Samarkand. The narrator sets the stable — and by extension the city — ablaze as he escapes at dawn. This scene directly dramatizes the historical Bolgar sack covered later in Ch.1's "The Golden Horde" section.
**Key themes:** East-vs-West vampire contact from the Eastern side; the Anda's alien, animalistic worldview (measuring time by sun/horizon, indifference to mortal hygiene and comfort) presented as a deliberate contrast to Western Kindred narration style.
**Key terms/entities:** Bolgar (Bulgar capital); Prince Hasan (unnamed Western vampire's mortal-facing cover ruler); Tengri and Itügen invoked as the narrator's gods.

## Introduction: A Starlit Hemisphere (pp. 10-15)
**Summary:** Opens with a Tacitus epigraph ("They make a desolation, and call it peace") framing the Mongols as the latest in a historical wave of steppe invasions of Europe (Ostrogoths, Huns, Avars). The "Hordes of the Khan" section (p.12) sets the book's scope (suggested chronicle timeframe 1167-1368, usable earlier/later too), establishes that at the Mongol Empire's height nearly every Cainite clan has representatives within it, and that the Mongol advance initially only threatens the Black Tortoise and White Tiger Wan Kuei Courts before eventually touching all Five August Courts. Introduces Clan Gangrel's discovery of the long-lost Anda bloodline and states the book's central theme: **"the bright candle"** — the Mongol Empire (and the Anda) burn twice as hot and half as long as ordinary history. Gives Mood guidance (energetic, up-tempo, not necessarily optimistic — the "Pax Mongolica" is a grim joke) and a Chapter by Chapter preview (p.12). A boxed sidebar, **"A Primer on the Middle Kingdom"** (p.13), gives a condensed *Kindred of the East* primer for readers unfamiliar with that line: Wan Kuei as mortals damned back from Hell rather than Caine's childer, the Dharma system (all five orthodox Dharmas named), chih-mei/disciple/"Running Monkey" terminology, the Five August Courts (Black Tortoise/north, White Tiger/west, Scarlet Phoenix/south, Azure Dragon/east, Yellow Emperor/center), and Yama Kings/akuma. Closes with an extensive **Lexicon** (p.13) of Mongol and Mongol-vampire terminology (anda, Anda, arban, Golden Horde, kuriltai, noker, oboq, Tengri, ulus, Wan Kuei, wu, Wu Zao, Xiong Ren, zhongren, etc.) and a **Bibliography** (p.15, magazines, books, film — including a wry note to root for the Huns while watching Disney's *Mulan* — and websites).
**Key themes:** Historical framing of Mongol invasion as one wave among many; explicit theme statement ("the bright candle"); orienting readers new to the Kindred of the East line.
**Key terms/entities:** "The bright candle" theme (p.12); Five August Courts primer (p.13); Lexicon (p.13); Bibliography (p.15).
**Notable claims/data points:** Suggested chronicle timeframe 1167-1368; explicit note that "Golden Horde" is used anachronistically for descriptive convenience and player characters shouldn't use the term in-character.

## Chapter One: Empire of the World-Conqueror (pp. 16-45)
**Summary:** The largest chapter — a full narrative history of the Mongol Empire interleaved with vampire politics. **Nomads of the East** (p.16) covers steppe life and tribal organization (ulus/oboq/khan/khagan/noker relationships). **Legendary Roots** (p.18) gives the Mongols' own origin legend (a blue-gray wolf and a fallow deer, their descendant Batachikhan, Dobun the Clever and Alan the Fair, youngest son Bodonchar founding the Borijigin clan from which Temujin descends) — with a sidebar, **"Vampiric Claims,"** noting that Anda vampires draw explicit parallels between this myth and their own (their founder-figure Dobrul the Brave as a parallel to Dobun; Duua the Blind's mythic third eye compared to the Wu Zao's literal third eye). Covers **Days and Nights on the Steppe** (p.19, food/drink incl. qumis and grut), **The Ordu and Ger** (p.20, camp structure and taboos, with a sidebar on **"Vampires in a Ger"** — a ger can function as minor holy ground against vampires), **Men and Women** (p.21, gender roles, with a sidebar on the rarity of **Female Anda**), **The Steppe Pony** and **Clothing and Weaponry** (p.22), a sidebar on **Nestorianism, Prester John and the Apocalypse** (p.22), and **Spirituality and Shamans** (p.23, with a sidebar, **"Shamanism by the Numbers,"** noting True Faith is nearly as rare among Mongol shamans as among mortal priests). **Cainites in the Ancient East** (p.23) recounts how Western Cainites have long avoided the Far East, fearing "Giants of the East" and demons — and reveals they were right to fear it: the **Anda**, an offshoot of Clan Gangrel who have lost most memory of their descent from Caine, hide among the Mongol tribes, while the **Wan Kuei** ("Demons of Xia" to the Anda) actively hunt them. A sidebar, **"Other Orphans"** (p.24), introduces the **Wu Zao** in brief. **Monstrous Legendry** (p.24) gives the Anda's own origin myth: the earth goddess Itügen and sky god Tengri came from the "Sunset Lands" with a large divine family, were attacked by demons of the Xia Empire, and Itügen's son **Dobrul the Brave** held a pass to let his family escape, apparently dying — but a Xia shaman's curse instead transformed him into the first vampire, craving mortal blood; Dobrul went on to create the Anda bloodline from his own blood, warning all his childer to beware the Xia Demons. **Tribal Unlife** (p.25) covers the *yasun* (herd-tribe) relationship and an **Enemies** section (p.25, the Tatar Beast Court of Xiong Ren, and above all the Black Tortoise Wan Kuei, whose "chief catspaw" in 1197 is the Tatar tribe). **The Tribes of the Khans** (p.26) covers 1197-era Mongol politics: the Temujin/Jamugha rivalry (former *anda* brothers), Toghril Khan of the Kerayit, the generations-old Tatar/Mongol blood feud, and the Merkid, Naiman, Onggut, Kerayit, and Qipchaq tribes — with a sidebar, **The Anda Bond** (p.26), explaining the mortal *anda* blood-brotherhood custom (often including a sexual dimension) that the vampire bloodline's Embrace practices deliberately mirror. **Cainites in the Old Kingdoms of the East** (p.28) covers Assamite, Gangrel, and Ravnos activity in Samarkand and Bukhara (including the Assamite vizier Karim of Samarkand, cursed with lost sense of touch), followed by capsule political histories of **Kara-Khitai**, **Khwarazm**, **Xixia**, **Jin**, and **Song** China (pp.28-29). **The Rise of the World-Conqueror (1206-1227)** (p.30) narrates Temujin's harsh youth, his father Yesugei's murder by Tatars, the kidnapping and rescue of his wife Börte, and the 1206 kuriltai naming him Genghis Khan. **The Army of the Great Khan** (p.30) explains Mongol military organization (arban/jagun/minghan/tumen decimal units, the *nerge* mass hunt as training) with a sidebar on **The Great Yasa** (Mongol law code, administered by judge Shigi-Qutuqu) and **Predators Among the Horde** (Anda riding with the armies; other Cainite clans beginning to Embrace Mongols as the empire grows). **The Campaigns** (p.31) and **Chinese Conquests** (p.32) cover early Mongol difficulty with siege warfare and the conquest of Xixia and Jin, with a **Communications** sidebar on the Yam courier relay system. A boxed note, **"The Anda and Wan Kuei"** (p.33), describes early, one-sided Anda incursions into Wan Kuei-controlled cities (Daidu, Shangdu, Ningxia) ending in slaughter of the Anda by the Black Tortoise Court. **Kara-Khitai and Khwarazm** (p.33) covers Kuchlug's fall and Muhammad's Khwarazm collapsing before the Mongol advance (with a **Taxation** sidebar on Great Yasa-era fiscal policy) and **Other Targets** (p.33, Jebe and Subetai's reconnaissance raid through the Caucasus and southern Russia). A full **Timeline of the Mongol Era** table (p.34, covering 1166-1502) follows. **Interlude: Genghis' Death and Succession (1227-1241)** (p.35) covers Genghis Khan's 1227 death (with a sidebar, **"The Great Khan After Death,"** noting no vampire is known to have Embraced him despite Anda rumors to the contrary) and Ogadei's succession. **The Mongols and Europe** (p.35) frames medieval Europe's confused perception of the Mongols (the Prester John legend, "Tartar"/Tartarus wordplay), with a sidebar, **"A Plea for Aid,"** on the 1238 Ismaili assassin missions to European courts. **European Affairs** and **European Cainites** (p.36) cover the political landscape (Frederick II, Pope Gregory IX, Bela IV of Hungary, Henry of Silesia) and clan-by-clan Cainite responses (Tzimisce war-preparation, Tremere/Ceoris politics, a sidebar on the **Knights of Prophecy**'s oracular severed head predicting the invasion in 1206, plus Cappadocian, Toreador, Malkavian, and Assamite reactions, with a **"The Unlikely Responses"** sidebar cautioning Storytellers against unrealistic unified vampire mobilization). **The Golden Horde** and **Vampires Among the Horde** (p.38) narrate the 1236 sack of Bolgar (the event dramatized in the opening fiction) and the Russian campaign, with a **Princes of Russia** sidebar on Russian Cainite princes' fatal paralysis, and a survey of which vampires ride with the army — Anda dominate numerically, alongside Wu Zao, the ancient Gangrel Arnulf (a former companion of the Huns) and his new Mongol childer, Ravnos, Assamites, displaced Ventrue and Tzimisce, and three named *wu* of Black Tortoise Wan Kuei (the Valiant Archers, the Dragon's Ember, and the Silver Blade Horsemen) — with a **Cainite Agendas** sidebar contrasting Arnulf's civilization-destroying agenda against Batu and Subetai's simple conquest goals and the Anda's mere curiosity. **Invasion** (p.39), **Europe's Answer** (p.40), the Wahlstadt/Liegnitz and Sajó River battles, and **Withdrawal** (p.41, triggered by Ogadei's death) cover the 1241 Hungarian campaign, with a **"The Death of Ogadei"** sidebar (p.42) surveying Cainite conspiracy theories (Assamites, Tremere, and Gangrel all suspected). **Divisions and Successions** (p.42) covers Guyuk's and Mongke's reigns, with a sidebar on the real historical friar **Giovanni de Plano Carpini**'s 1245-46 mission to Karakorum. **Persia** (p.43) covers Hulegu's conquest and the 1258 sack of Baghdad, with an **"Alamut and Aluh Amut"** sidebar distinguishing the mortal Ismaili fortress from the true, hidden Assamite Alamut. **Kublai Khan** (p.43) covers the conquest of Song China, with a **"Conflict Behind the Scenes"** sidebar revealing that the Ariq-boke/Kublai civil war conceals a proxy war between Yellow Emperor and Black Tortoise Wan Kuei factions. **The Sun Sets** (p.45) closes the chapter with the Yuan Dynasty's decline, the 1368 Ming overthrow, and Karakorum's final destruction in 1388.
**Key themes:** Vampiric politics as a hidden layer beneath recorded history; the Anda's precarious, largely unwelcome existence in a world dominated by hostile Wan Kuei; Storyteller-facing caution against over-neat, unified Cainite responses to history.
**Key terms/entities:** Dobrul the Brave (p.24); Itügen and Tengri origin myth (p.24); Anda Bond (p.26); Timeline of the Mongol Era (p.34); Golden Horde/Bolgar sack (p.38); Valiant Archers/Dragon's Ember/Silver Blade Horsemen (p.38); Arnulf (p.39); Giovanni de Plano Carpini (p.42); Alamut/Aluh Amut distinction (p.43).

## Chapter Two: Monsters on the Steppe (pp. 46-65)
**Summary:** The bestiary/bloodline chapter. **On Mongols and Vampires** (p.47) frames why the Mongol steppe was historically unattractive vampiric territory (low, dispersed mortal population) until conquest made it a magnet for ambitious Cainites and Cathayans alike. **The Scions of Caine** (p.47) surveys clan-by-clan involvement: **Assamites** (viziers and sorcerers drift out of clan politics onto the steppe), **Baali** (many believe Genghis Khan's tribes are the Biblical Gog/Magog and seek to join the "apocalypse"), **Brujah** (Zealot elders see a chance to rebuild a new Carthage in the invasion's wake), **Cappadocians** (drawn to Mongol-caused mass death sites; the Giovanni bloodline later appears at Kublai's Shangdu court), **Followers of Set** (initially ignore the Far East as too vast and dangerous, later claim false credit for the Mamluks' Ain Jalut victory), **Gangrel** (split between resisting the invaders and reveling in the chaos; first Western clan to meet the Anda, treated as long-lost relatives), **Lasombra** (focus on the Church's fortunes, mostly avoid the Mongol khanates directly), **Malkavians**, **Nosferatu**, **Ravnos** (embrace outcast Mongols and Qipchaqs), **Salubri** (the Western remnant occasionally quests east, largely lost or transformed by Eastern philosophy), **Toreador** (drawn by novel Mongol art, turned back at Kashgar by Cathayan vampires), **Tremere** (plumb toppled Islamic sultanates for magical secrets, avoid Cathay as too dangerous), **Tzimisce** (badly savaged by the invasion, unable to integrate into nomadic society), **Ventrue** (young Patricians carve out estates in the khanates), and **Minor Bloodlines** (enslaved Gargoyles occasionally escape amid the chaos; Laibon, Lamia, and Lhiannan largely uninvolved) — this survey runs pp.47-51. Full writeups follow for the book's two new bloodlines and the era's dominant Wan Kuei court: **Anda** (p.52) — appearance, havens/prey, background, character creation notes, bloodline Disciplines (Animalism, Fortitude, Protean), the unique curse (cannot sleep more than three nights in the same one-mile area without escalating dice-pool penalties), organization via *kuriltai*, and their historical **Destiny** — rising to prominence under Kublai Khan, then hunted to extinction by the Wan Kuei of China by 1388. **Wu Zao** (p.54) — an Eastern offshoot of Clan Salubri descended from Zao-lat (the Wu Zao's name for Saulot), organized into scholar (descended from Zao-xue) and thief (descended from Zao-zei) castes that typically pair up, bloodline Disciplines (Auspex, Fortitude, Valeren), and a compulsive research weakness tied to Zao-lat's dying imperative to uncover hidden knowledge of the coming Ages of Sorrow and Darkness. **Demons of Xia: The Black Tortoise Court** (p.56) is the chapter's centerpiece — the fullest non-*Kindred of the East*-line treatment of this court anywhere in this library. Covers its **History** (three founding *wu* — the Regretful Hunters, Riders on Ashen Plain, Sons of Tengri — and the destruction of half the court's founders when Anda burned the court hall Qaragha in retaliation for a massacre, the root of the Anda/Black Tortoise blood feud), **The Supernatural Landscape** (p.57, the Great Wall's dragon-line disruption, "pit" dragon nests of corrupt Chi), a sidebar on the Yama King **Dokhor-Khan and the Hell of Spiked Chains** (p.58, the demon lord most responsible for corrupting the Black Tortoise Court's northern dragon nests), **Traditions and Culture** (p.58, biweekly *kuriltai* gatherings, the point-of-honor custom of ignoring weather), **Prominent Corpse Families** (p.59, the Barren Wind, Black Hoof Riders, and Storm of Fire *wu*), **Protocol** (p.60, the *zhongren* go-between tradition for requesting favors, with a worked example), a sidebar on rumors that **Genghis Khan** himself returned as a Wan Kuei after death (p.61), **Relations with Others** (p.61-62, ties to the White Tiger, Yellow Emperor, Azure Dragon, and Scarlet Phoenix Courts, and to **The Beast Courts of the North** — the Tatar Beast Court under Beast King Bogabo, with a live succession dispute between the Victorious Strike and Autumn Snow camps), a note on the *hsien*'s minimal contact with the court (p.63), **Wan Kuei in the West** (p.63, the *kanbujian* — half-foreign Wan Kuei — and the Scarlet Night Daggers *wu*'s cold experiments on captured Western vampires), **Akuma** (p.63, corruption rates rising from roughly 1-in-12 Black Tortoise Wan Kuei in 1197 to 1-in-2 by the time of the Quincunx, driven mainly by Yama King Dokhon-Khan), **Destruction of the Anda** (p.64, the court's ultimate solution to the Anda "problem" — betrayed by a Wu Zao informant, the Wan Kuei locate the resting place of Dobrul the Brave himself and use his captured body to summon and destroy his descendants en masse during the 1388 fall of Karakorum), and **The Fifth Age** (p.64, closing with the Black Tortoise Court's internal schism between factions who sign the 1304 Treaty of the Quincunx and a renunciate faction who reject both Heaven and Hell — and the court's ultimate dissolution as the other August Courts march on its corrupted remnants).
**Key themes:** The Anda and Wu Zao as marginal, hunted creatures compared to the organized, ancient power of the Black Tortoise Court; the Mongol era as the high-water mark and beginning of the end for the Anda bloodline specifically.
**Key terms/entities:** Anda bloodline (p.52); Wu Zao bloodline (p.54); Black Tortoise Court/Qaragha (p.56); Dokhor-Khan (p.58); Barren Wind/Black Hoof Riders/Storm of Fire *wu* (p.59); Zhongren protocol (p.60); Tatar Beast Court/Bogabo (p.62); Kanbujian (p.63); Destruction of the Anda (p.64).

## Chapter Three: Gifts of the Sky and Earth (pp. 66-85)
**Summary:** The systems chapter. **Creating Mongol Characters** (p.68) covers Abilities (Skills favored over Talents/Knowledges), Disciplines (Gangrel's inherent gifts — Animalism, Fortitude, Protean — prioritized for Anda; sorcery and Dominate largely unavailable without a foreign teacher), and notes that Wan Kuei of the Black Tortoise Court have access to the Godbody Disciplines of Earth and Fire (from *World of Darkness: Blood & Silk*) as an especially fitting reflection of their Itügen/Tengri devotion. **Backgrounds** (p.69) gives Mongol-specific guidance (Allies for an *anda* brother, Generation minimums — Anda no lower than 5th, Wu Zao all 10th or lower, Herd tied to camp life, Mentor built around the *noker* relationship, Nushi guardian spirits for Wan Kuei only, Resources measured in ger/wives/horses rather than gold). **Merits and Flaws** (p.69) flags Mongol-appropriate and inappropriate choices, and **Roads** (p.69) discusses which Roads fit Mongol Cainites (Heaven and the Roads of Paradox/Typhon poorly; Humanity commonly; Beast and Chivalry both partially, synthesized into the new **Road of the Yasa**). **New Traits** introduces Anda-specific Discipline powers called **"Ma"** (p.70): **Steal the Terrible Swiftness** (Animalism 2/Fortitude 1, borrowed steppe-pony speed on foot), **Shared Strength** (Animalism 3/Protean 2, a blood-bond with one's mount granting boosted Attributes/speed), and **Itügen's Embrace** (Animalism 1/Protean 3, extending earth-sleep protection to a ghoul or childe sharing the vampire's grave). **Black Tortoise Rites** (p.72) gives six new ritual systems: Speak Through the Tongue of the Dragon (Level One, spirit-courier messaging), Sense of the Ger (Level One, tracking a specific ger's location), Encircle (Level Three, homing enchanted arrows), Strength of the Herd (Level Three, consuming a steppe pony's blood for temporary Strength), and Commune with Tengri / Commune with Itügen (both Level Four, communion rites for insight or gifts of fertility/strength) — the Tengri rite includes a sidebar clarifying that Genghis Khan's own historical communion ritual came from mortal shamanic tradition, not this Wan Kuei rite. **The Road of the Yasa** (p.75) is detailed as a new Road built around Barbarian ethics — protecting family and *anda* brothers above all, honesty and discipline as paramount virtues, but no Western battlefield "honor" (any tactic that wins is acceptable) — with Virtues Conviction and Self-Control and a full Hierarchy of Sins table. New Merits/Flaws follow: **Inoffensive to Animals**, **Sense of the Stock** (detecting supernatural creatures via animal-like unease), and **Repulsive to Animals** (p.76). **Spirits of Nature** (p.76) profiles sample steppe spirits (Dara of the Upper Onon River, Tzakok a *ger* fire spirit) and discusses Tengri and Itügen themselves as distant, rarely-personified cosmic forces. **Steppe Systems** (p.77) gives **Basic Profiles** for a Mongol Horseman and Mongol Warhorse, notes on **True Faith**'s rarity among Mongols (roughly 1 *ger* in 100 has any True Faith at all), and a full **Warfare** section (p.78) covering Combat from Horseback (mounted Archery rules, a Basic Horseplay sidebar), **Sacking a City** (p.79, siege/sack mechanics), **Travel**, **Fording a River**, **Finding Food on the Steppe** (p.80), **Finding Game**, and **Vampires and Animal Blood** (p.81, mechanical penalties for relying on animal blood long-term). **Storytelling and the Mongol Horde** (p.82) tackles **The Problem of Inevitability** — balancing historical fatalism against player agency — with four named techniques (**Deviate**, **Obfuscate**, **Distract**, **Cooperate**) and **Chronicle Options** (p.83) covering Mongol Coteries and Wu (reasons Anda groups stay together — War, the Hunt, Camaraderie, Embrace, the *Noker* bond) and four chronicle templates: **The Western Chronicle** (p.84, passive Cainites in a besieged Eastern European/Asian city), **The Eastern Chronicle** (p.85, Mongol vampire protagonists across the whole imperial arc), **The Crossover Chronicle** (p.85, mixed Anda/Wan Kuei troupes on the high steppe), and **The World of Darkness Chronicle** (p.85, a wide-open multi-gameline romp, with an explicit caution that this option risks losing thematic focus).
**Key themes:** Mechanical codification of nomadic vampire existence; Storyteller-facing guidance on historicity and player agency, the most extensive such discussion in this library.
**Key terms/entities:** Ma powers (p.70); Black Tortoise Rites (p.72-74); Road of the Yasa (p.75); Basic Profiles (p.77); The Problem of Inevitability (p.82); Chronicle Options (p.83-85).

## Appendix: Khans of the Night (pp. 86-96)
**Summary:** Ready-to-play templates and prominent NPCs. Five templates: **Steppe Warrior** (p.88, a young, newly-adult Anda warrior, contemptuous of soft Western vampires), **Temple Thief** (p.89, a Wu Zao thief-caste vampire in service to the scholar Xing Tai, searching for dark secrets about the coming apocalypse), **Unliving Scout** (p.90, a female Ravnos scout who broke gender norms in life and was Embraced by a wandering Gangrel elder after her Mongol sire fled south), **Wise Woman** (p.91, an elder-seeming female Anda, formerly her camp's shaman, Embraced by a sire who told her nothing of her new nature before fleeing), and **Yam Courier** (p.92, a mortal courier crippled in a riding accident and given the Embrace and use of his legs back by a passing Anda). Five named NPCs follow under **Favored of the Spirits** (p.93): **Jelme the Damned, Guardian of the Khan** (an Anda grandchilde of Dobrul who serves as Genghis Khan's secret, unacknowledged bodyguard — he once gave Genghis his own healing blood in battle and has felt guilty over the implied blood-bond ever since); **Aajav, the Young Wolf** (p.93, a Mongol warrior Embraced by the Gangrel elder Oderic after a dispute with a local Anda group over who would claim him — later diablerized by his own childe Qarakh around 1236); **Chinkhai the Fierce, Judge of the Steppe** (p.94, an elder Anda grandson of Dobrul who guards his sire's tomb and dispenses harsh, often lethal justice at *kuriltai*); **Silat ibn Isa, Vizier of Khwarazm** (p.95, a devout Muslim Assamite vizier in Bukhara, driven into a one-Cainite guerrilla war against the Mongols after the sack of his city, convinced vampires of East and West are conspiring against Clan Assamite and Islam); and **Bortai the Fair, Bone Dancer** (p.95, one of nine maidens Genghis Khan kept — and, unrevealed to her at the time, was fated to be ritually sacrificed alongside — at his funeral in 1227; she rose from the tomb as a newly-made, half-mad Wan Kuei and was recovered by a Black Tortoise Court *wu*; she is now the only living being who knows the secret location of Genghis Khan's tomb).
**Key themes:** Player-ready hooks tying directly back into Ch.1's historical narrative (Jelme and Genghis Khan; Bortai and the Khan's secret tomb; Silat ibn Isa and the sack of Bukhara).
**Key terms/entities:** Jelme the Damned (p.93); Aajav the Young Wolf (p.93); Chinkhai the Fierce (p.94); Silat ibn Isa (p.95); Bortai the Fair (p.95).

## Cross-cutting themes
- **"The bright candle"**: the book's explicit stated theme (Introduction, p.12) — recurs as the Anda's entire arc across Ch.1 (rising to prominence under Kublai) and Ch.2 (hunted to extinction by 1388), and again in Ch.3's Storytelling advice to reinforce the theme through short-lived, brilliant characters.
- **The Anda/Black Tortoise blood feud**: the single most-referenced conflict in the book, established mythologically in Ch.1 (Dobrul's origin, the Xia Demons) and given full political and historical treatment in Ch.2 (the burning of Qaragha, the century-spanning grudge, and its endpoint in the Anda's engineered extinction).
- **History as vampire-politics palimpsest**: nearly every major historical event in Ch.1 (the Ariq-boke/Kublai civil war, Ogadei's death, Genghis Khan's own afterlife) is given a hidden vampiric explanation, reinforcing the book's core conceit that recorded history conceals a vampiric substructure.
- **Historicity vs. player agency**: uniquely among this library's books, Ch.3 devotes substantial space to explicit Storyteller-facing techniques (Deviate/Obfuscate/Distract/Cooperate) for reconciling a chronicle's fixed historical endpoint (the Golden Horde will sack Kiev; Ogadei will die in 1241) with meaningful player choice.
